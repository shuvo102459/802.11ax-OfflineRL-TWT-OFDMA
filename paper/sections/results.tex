\subsection{The two mechanisms do not combine}

Table~\ref{tab:main} holds three pairs of rows differing only in the OFDMA bit,
and in every pair enabling uplink OFDMA costs energy: $+62.8\,\%$ under the
static schedule, $+33.6\,\%$ under random TWT, $+8.3\,\%$ with TWT off.

Throughput falls in all three pairs, so this is not a throughput-for-energy
trade: the OFDMA arms are worse on both axes.

The ordering is the informative part: the penalty is largest where TWT sleeps
hardest and smallest where it does not sleep at all, because soliciting a
station competes with the schedule trying to keep it asleep.

\subsection{The cost is carrier sensing, not lost sleep}

Our first explanation was that triggers wake sleeping stations. The traces
refute it. Mean sleep occupancy---the fraction of the control window a station
spends in PHY sleep, averaged over the sixteen---differs by under $0.8$
percentage points between the OFDMA arms. Stations sleep just as much; their
awake time simply costs more.

The PHY-state traces show where it goes. Of the $113.7$\,J of extra energy,
$69\,\%$ is carrier-sense busy and $24\,\%$ receiving; only $9\,\%$ is
transmission. A solicited station listens through the trigger, the allocation
and the acknowledgement, charged at the CCA-busy current.

This also tells us what the magnitude depends on. The scenario inherits ns-3's
default CCA-busy current of $273$\,mA while setting its own receive current to
$66$\,mA. Repricing CCA at the receive current cuts the extra energy from
$113.7$ to $49.8$\,J, still a cost rather than a saving. Re-running a matched
pair under the repriced model, the learned policy's advantage over the same
hand-designed controller narrows from $31.4$ to $19.9\,\%$, both at
$p = 1.8\times10^{-15}$. Signs survive the change; magnitudes do not.

\subsection{One algorithm beats the tuned rule}

Given a free choice of OFDMA mode the tuned controller disables it, worth
$99$\,J ($265.5$ against $166.5$\,J) at slightly better throughput. We take the
stronger form, $166.5$\,J at $19.22$\,Mb/s, as the baseline.


IQL reaches $107.3 \pm 2.6$\,J over ten fits: a $35.6\,\%$ reduction, CI
$[-36.5,-34.7]\,\%$, $p = 1.8\times10^{-15}$ on fifty paired seeds. Nothing is
traded for it: throughput rises to $20.76$ from $19.22$\,Mb/s and mean delay
falls by $83$\,ms ($p = 9.8\times10^{-9}$ paired), while the change in budget
violations is not statistically detectable ($p = 0.07$). Its worst fit of ten,
$112.4$\,J, still beats every other method's best.

Absolute delays warrant a word: the cell carries a persistent standing queue,
flat at $2.6$--$2.9$\,s across all $42$ decisions and across every arm,
including the one with neither mechanism. It reflects queue depth under this
offered load rather than a response to scheduling, so we read delay as a guard
against winning by starving traffic, not as a latency claim.

\subsection{Four of five do not, and selection is why}

Table~\ref{tab:main} is the reason we claim this for IQL and not for offline
RL as a family. Read the \emph{typical fit} column, which compares the mean over
the ten fits and so describes what training once yields. DQN$+$CQL is
$15.9\,\%$ worse than the hand-designed rule ($p = 8.6\times10^{-5}$) and
dueling DQN$+$CQL is $16.6\,\%$ worse ($p = 4.3\times10^{-6}$). DDQN and
behaviour cloning are worse still.

Now read the \emph{best fit} column. There the two CQL variants sit within
$1.6\,\%$ of the baseline, and neither difference is statistically detectable:
$p = 0.74$ and $p = 0.55$, with the paired difference's confidence interval
spanning zero. The gap between the two columns is the whole problem. A
practitioner who trains once gets a policy $16\,\%$ worse than a hand-written
rule. A practitioner who can identify the best of ten gets a tie. Identifying
it requires measuring all ten on the network, which is the cost offline RL
exists to avoid.

IQL is the exception because it barely varies: its across-fit standard deviation
is $2.6$\,J against $10.1$ to $14.7$\,J for the others, so which fit you deploy
does not matter.

\subsection{What separates the methods is the OFDMA bit}

The five methods order by energy exactly as they order by how often they enable
the scheduler (Fig.~\ref{fig:ordering}): IQL $0\,\%$ through behaviour cloning
at $48\,\%$---the last because the logging policy chose the OFDMA bit by coin
flip, and imitating it reproduces that.

The method that learned to stop soliciting is the one that wins. The coordinate
search concluded the same by a different route---a staged grid over thresholds
rather than value iteration over $760$ actions---so two optimisers of different
character agree that at this operating point the mechanisms substitute. The obvious objection is that we crippled the scheduler: ns-3 solicits four
stations per trigger by default, while schedule~11 leaves eight awake and the
channel admits nine resource units, so half the awake set was unreachable.
Raising the limit to nine makes uplink OFDMA worse, not better---$+87.4\,\%$
against the no-OFDMA arm instead of $+62.8\,\%$ under the static schedule, and
$+83.3\,\%$ instead of $+59.5\,\%$ for the hand-designed controller, both
$+15\,\%$ on the default at $p = 2\times10^{-15}$ with throughput falling at
each step. Solicitation cost scales with the stations a trigger names; the
benefit scales with the data they have, and in this mix they have little.

\subsection{The result survives the load change}

Each seed either holds its offered load constant or steps it by $1.5$ to
$2.5\times$ partway through. Splitting the fifty on that flag tests whether the
policy needs a settled traffic pattern; it does not---$35.1\,\%$ below the
baseline on the $19$ constant-load seeds ($p = 3.8\times10^{-6}$) and
$35.8\,\%$ on the $31$ stepped ones ($p = 9.3\times10^{-10}$), with higher
throughput in both.

\subsection{What the measurement rests on}

Four instrumentation faults each produced the opposite headline, and all four
were invisible in aggregate statistics. A convergence gate admitted only
snapshots whose FQE drift fell below $2\,\%$; none passed, so selection fell
back to the final epoch, which both FQE and the simulator rank last of four.
Evaluating every snapshot in ns-3 gives a Spearman correlation of $-1.000$
between FQE value and energy, so the ranking was sound and the gate discarded
it, at $6.5\,\%$ energy; we now rank on FQE alone. The inherited reward charged
$\tanh(E/E_0)$ at $E_0=400$\,mJ against an $E$ spanning $1390$ to
$14{,}167$\,mJ, saturated for $99.99\,\%$ of transitions; retraining all ten
fits on it costs $13.3$\,J ($120.7 \pm 5.6$ against $107.3 \pm 2.6$,
$p = 3.6\times10^{-15}$ paired and $1.2\times10^{-4}$ across fits), though it
still beats the baseline by $27.5\,\%$, so the repair improves the result
rather than producing it. Two harnesses measured different windows, one
including a warm-up burning $26.6\,\%$ of episode energy in $9.8\,\%$ of it;
matched to the $42$-decision window they agree to $4.5\,\%$. Finally the
scenario deferred each PHY sleep by $8$\,ms with the OFDMA bit set,
worth $+62.0\,\%$ on its own---two thirds of the $+92.5\,\%$ we had charged to
the protocol. Sleep occupancy moved by under $0.8\,\%$ between those arms, which
is why we first read it as harmless: it changed when stations slept, not how
much. Service-period alignment removes the race, and every OFDMA arm here runs
without the deferral.
